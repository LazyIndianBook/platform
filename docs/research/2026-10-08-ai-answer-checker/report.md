# AI answer-sheet checker for ExamLeaf: OCR for handwritten Assamese, Bengali, Hindi and Eng…

No OCR or grading system has been tested on ExamLeaf's actual input: phone photos of Assamese, Bengali or Hindi answer sheets with maths and diagrams. The nearest results are poor. The best published score for handwritten Assamese is 71.6% word accuracy (Gemini 3.1 Pro, on a benchmark run by a vendor). Word error on handwritten Hindi and Bengali pages is 3-7% on clean prose with Sarvam OCR or Gemini Flash, and 17-25% on another benchmark. AWS Textract and Azure cannot read Assamese or Bengali, and Google Document AI does not list Assamese. On handwriting, the Chinese OCR specialists (DeepSeek-OCR, PaddleOCR-VL, MinerU, dots.ocr) score below general vision models, even in Chinese and English. Every generative reader also silently 'corrects' student mistakes: 63-79% of errors are of this kind, and 88-95% on formulas. Long handwritten maths is read badly by every model tested, so a transcript can turn a wrong answer into a right one before it is marked. On OpenRouter the best-value Chinese vision model is GLM-5.3-Flash ($0.15/$0.50 per million input/output tokens, $0.06/$0.20 in batch, Artificial Analysis index 41.8). Gemini 3.8 Flash scores 40.9 at $0.75/$3.75. However, Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna cost the same as GLM-5.3-Flash ($0.10/$0.50), and no model has checked evidence on grading accuracy or Assamese. Gemini 2.5 and most Qwen3-VL listings expire this month. OpenRouter's default routing can send children's answer sheets to providers that store data or train on it, unless zdr:true and an allow-list of providers are set. Working from checked prices, the AI cost is about ₹0.2-₹17 per 12-page paper (US$0.002-0.18). The suggested pipeline costs about ₹5.5 per paper, or ₹55-₹170 per student for 20 papers a year. Reading the pages (Sarvam charges ₹0.50 per page) and teacher review cost more than the grading itself. A careful first version would be: practice-only and two-stage, with a word-for-word transcript the student can see; routing restricted to zero-retention endpoints; teacher review of low-confidence answers; a head-to-head test against Sarvam Vision 2.1 and the open IndicOCR model on 100-200 of ExamLeaf's own teacher-marked papers before launch; and a lawyer's review of parental consent under India's data protection law (the DPDP Act) before children's handwriting is sent abroad.

## Findings

### 1. [medium] Handwritten Assamese has never been measured on exam answer sheets. The closest evidence puts word error at about 28-34% for the best general systems on handwriting blocks, and about 15% for a recogniser trained for the job on isolated word crops. Assamese reading should be treated as unproven.

**Evidence:** Bodhan AI and AI4Bharat run their own IndicOCR-HW benchmark. The figures are self-reported; the model card was last modified 7 Sep 2026. Their blog describes 10,544 pre-cropped text-block images across 12 Indian languages plus English, with writers from school students to adults. The benchmark is not released and has no exam sheets. Word accuracy is 100 x (1 - WER). Assamese results: Gemini 3.1 Pro 71.6, the open 0.8B IndicOCR 66.1, Sarvam Vision 47.8, Gemma-4-31B 24.1, Chandra OCR 2 8.9, Surya OCR 2 17.8. Gemini 3.1 Pro vs IndicOCR in other languages: Bengali 74.8/71.3, Hindi 83.1/77.6, English 84.4/80.7, overall 72.0/66.7. IndicOCR reads printed Assamese at 90.2, so handwriting is much harder. Gemini beats IndicOCR on 12 of 13 handwriting rows. The Sarvam Vision column predates Sarvam Vision 2.1 (24 Sep 2026), the release that added Indic handwriting, so that figure is likely out of date (my inference from the dates). IIIT-Indic-HW-UC (ICPR 2024): an older-style baseline trained and tested on phone-camera word crops scores Assamese CRR 90.98 / WRR 85.15, Bengali 96.60/89.10 and Hindi 93.88/84.69. That is 11-15% word error even on data like its training data. The writers were adults copying short paragraphs, and the test split is not shown to use new writers. The IIIT page-level paper (IndicPage-OCR, DAS 2026) covers only Hindi, Bengali, Tamil and Telugu. It never mentions Assamese, maths, equations, diagrams or answer scripts, and lists mixed-script pages as future work. Sarvam's Vision 2.1 post claims handwriting support for English plus 22 Indic languages, including Assamese, but shows only demo videos and no accuracy figure. Assamese uses the Bengali Unicode block but has its own letters (U+09F0, U+09F1), so Bengali results are only a rough stand-in.

**Sources:** https://huggingface.co/bodhan-ai/indic-ocr, https://bodhan.ai/research/blogs/indic-ocr, https://cvit.iiit.ac.in/usodi/ucciohd.php, https://cvit.iiit.ac.in/images/ConferencePapers/2026_01/IndicPage-OCR.pdf, https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/sarvam-vision-2-1

### 2. [medium] On handwritten Hindi and Bengali prose pages, Sarvam OCR and Gemini Flash lead with 3-7% word error, and an open GOT-OCR model adapted with LoRA trails by 1-5 WER points. Another benchmark puts the best handwritten Hindi/Bengali WER at 17-25% with a different order of systems. Absolute error rates and vendor rankings do not carry over between datasets.

**Evidence:** IndicPage-OCR (Bhattacharyya, Mondal and Jawahar, IIIT Hyderabad; DAS 2026, Springer LNCS pp. 317-333) tests 500 pages per language from in-house data. WER/CER results: Sarvam OCR: Hindi 0.0682/0.0419, Bengali 0.0542/0.0194, Tamil 0.0382/0.0189, Telugu 0.0331/0.0221. Gemini Flash (version not stated): 0.0708/0.0447, 0.0584/0.0211, 0.0428/0.0207, 0.0377/0.0246. IndicPage-OCR: 0.0823/0.0466, 0.0719/0.0221, 0.0855/0.0266, 0.0426/0.0292. Gemini trails Sarvam by 0.3-0.5 WER points and IndicPage-OCR trails by 1.0-4.7. The abstract's 'gap cut about 80%' comes from a single example page (Fig. 1), not from the tables. The per-language adapters are public (Apache-2.0), but the test set is unreleased and the code is 'coming soon'. A verifier also read Table 1's zero-shot Bengali rows: Chandra 0.36 WER and DeepSeek-OCR (v1) 7.0 WER, meaning runaway output. A broader claim that zero-shot Chinese open models are 'unusable' on Indic pages failed verification (0-3), so treat this as one data point only. Bodhan's benchmark has a different order. Hindi: Gemini 3.1 Pro 83.1, IndicOCR 77.6, Sarvam Vision 72.3. Bengali: 74.8, 71.3, 58.3 (word accuracy). The same IIIT authors' ICDAR 2025 paper puts Google OCR at WRR 73.27 on handwritten Hindi words, below their adapted model at 75.21. So 'commercial leads' holds for the newer LLM-based engines, not for all commercial OCR.

**Sources:** https://cvit.iiit.ac.in/images/ConferencePapers/2026_01/IndicPage-OCR.pdf, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-36207-0_19, https://huggingface.co/bodhan-ai/indic-ocr

### 3. [high] Among commercial OCR APIs, only Sarvam Vision and Google's products (Gemini, Cloud Vision, Document AI) are realistic options for Assamese, Bengali and Hindi. AWS Textract and Azure essentially cannot read Assamese or Bengali. Google Document AI's handwriting OCR covers Bengali and Hindi but does not list Assamese. On its own printed-text benchmark, Sarvam leads Gemini Flash by only 1.4-3.3 points in the three target languages.

**Evidence:** Sarvam built and ran the Indic OCR Bench itself. It uses printed text blocks from textbooks, newspapers and magazines dated 1800 to the present: 6,909 samples, including 471 Assamese, 271 Bengali and 228 Hindi. Scores are word accuracy for Assamese / Bengali / Hindi. Sarvam Vision 2.1: 90.88/93.47/93.52. Bodhan IndicOCR: 89.41/90.87/90.99. Gemini 3.6 Flash: 87.55/90.36/92.11. Claude Opus 5: 86.62/88.75/89.38. Surya OCR 2: 87.25/81.79/86.12. Google Cloud Vision: 86.58/85.24/84.29. Azure Vision 4.0: 0.22/8.24/85.72. AWS Textract: 0.02/7.56/9.67. Overall, Sarvam scores 87.39 against Gemini 3.6 Flash's 79.35, but Manipuri alone accounts for about 3.8 of that gap. Gemini 3.7 and 3.8 Flash were not tested, and there is no handwriting subset. The benchmark is public under Apache-2.0 with a scoring script (metrics.py). It has been rewritten twice: 20,267 samples, then 16,658, then 6,909. Pin commit 84ce7ce before re-running it. Vendor documentation agrees: Textract handwriting is English-only, and Azure lists Hindi printed text but not Bengali or Assamese, with no Indic handwriting. Google Document AI Enterprise Document OCR is generally available, with regions including asia-south1 (Mumbai); the page was updated 5 Oct 2026. Handwriting is 'Supported' for Bengali, Hindi, Marathi, Nepali and English, and not for Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Punjabi, Tamil or Telugu. Assamese and Odia are missing from the 60-row table, and no accuracy figures are given. The price is $1.50 per 1,000 pages, about ₹1.74 per 12-page paper. Cloud Vision (updated 7 Oct 2026) marks Bengali-script and Devanagari handwriting, and Assamese, as 'Experimental', meaning not regularly evaluated. Sarvam pricing, read 8 Oct 2026: Digitisation API ₹0.50 per page (₹6 per paper) and Extraction API ₹1.00 per page. Digitise jobs are capped at 10 pages and 10 requests per minute. Sarvam models are not listed on OpenRouter, so they must be used through Sarvam's own API.

### 4. [medium] On handwriting benchmarks (Chinese, English and maths only, no Indic scripts), general-purpose vision models beat dedicated OCR models, including the Chinese OCR specialists DeepSeek-OCR, PaddleOCR-VL, MinerU, dots.ocr and HunyuanOCR. Qwen3-VL-8B led one benchmark and Gemini 3.1 Pro led the other.

**Evidence:** OmniHandwritingOCR (East China Normal University, arXiv 2608.18586, CIKM 2026) tested 13 systems on 77,572 images with one generic prompt. Results as unweighted mean 1-NED / CER / WER, where higher 1-NED is better: Qwen3-VL-8B 75.63/30.94/32.94; Kimi-VL-A3B 73.58/38.20/45.28; Qwen2.5-VL-72B 72.91/38.88/36.91; InternVL3-78B 72.16/32.19/34.72; Nanonets-OCR2-3B, the best specialist, 68.42/42.27/42.77; DeepSeek-OCR 64.27/73.84/69.94; PaddleOCR-VL 62.73/71.14/72.38; GPT-4o 59.32/48.53/50.03; GOT-OCR2 48.32/84.19; MinerU2.5-1.2B 21.33/125.59. On English text alone, Qwen3-VL-8B ties Nanonets and Qwen2.5-VL-72B at about 84.6-85.0. The specialists were not run with their own native prompts. WildHandBench (arXiv 2608.22959, v1 preprint) uses 500 real-world handwritten documents in Chinese and English. Overall scores: Gemini 3.1 Pro 71.85, Gemini 3.5 Flash 68.25, Kimi-K3 67.81, Qwen3.5-Plus 65.55, Qwen3-VL 62.19, Claude Opus 4.8 57.70; these six are all general models. The best of 11 OCR specialists are PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 (0.9B) 54.21, GLM-OCR 54.06, MinerU2.5-Pro 51.73, HunyuanOCR 51.44, dots.mocr 49.97 and dots.ocr 43.73. DeepSeek-OCR2 scores 9.46, probably a test-setup error: the appendix lists the v1 repository. On free text alone the gap is not uniform: HunyuanOCR (edit distance 38.50) and PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 (41.52) beat Claude Opus 4.8 (53.30). Counter-evidence: EDU-CIRCUIT-HW (ACL 2026 Findings) uses real STEM student handwriting. It ranks Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking last (85.43% sample error) and Gemini-3-Preview first (37.62%). A broader claim that Chinese open models sit close to the proprietary leaders failed verification (1-2).

**Sources:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18586, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22959, https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00095

### 5. [medium] Every model tested transcribes long, multi-line handwritten maths from real student answer sheets poorly. On the hard tier the best system reaches only 55.81 1-NED, with a character error rate of about 55%.

**Evidence:** OmniHandwritingOCR's multi-line tiers come from more than 6,000 real student maths answer sheets, written in Chinese plus LaTeX. From easy to hard, Qwen3-VL-8B's F1 falls from 86.92 to 70.45 and its CER rises from 24.95 to 57.08. Hard-tier 1-NED/CER: Qwen2.5-VL-72B 55.81/54.91, Qwen3-VL-8B 50.54/57.08, InternVL3-78B 45.59/60.80, DeepSeek-OCR 42.24/70.71, PaddleOCR-VL 41.78/83.51, Nanonets 41.67/67.51, GPT-4o 29.60/75.58. Mean answer length grows from 156.9 to 488.8 characters, LaTeX commands from 4.1 to 25.7, and lines from 3.7 to 13.0. Qualifications: scoring compares LaTeX strings, which penalises equivalent notation. GPT-4o's own errors helped define the hard tier. The dataset is not public. No Gemini, Claude or GPT-5-class model was tested. The direction is backed by Seong et al. (arXiv 2604.22774) and FERMAT (arXiv 2501.07244).

**Sources:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18586, https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22774, https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07244

### 6. [high] Generative readers 'fix' what the student actually wrote, so a transcription step can turn a wrong answer into a right one before marking. This happens across models and is worst on formulas.

**Evidence:** OmniHandwritingOCR (section 4.4) names four recurring errors: correcting the writer's mistakes, inserting plausible intermediate steps, dropping symbols that are hard to read, and drifting into Markdown or explanation. In Fig. 4, Qwen2.5-VL-72B and InternVL3-78B change a student's y^2/9 to y^2/3 and 1540 to 15400. Some character error rates exceed 100% (output longer than the original): DeepSeek-VL2-27B on Chinese text 133.85, DeepSeek-OCR on single-line formulas 154.81, PaddleOCR-VL 116.77, MinerU2.5 overall 125.59. The single generic prompt may exaggerate these. WildHandBench measures 'prior-driven' errors, where the model writes a more probable string than what is on the page, judged by Qwen3-8B-Base. For working models this is 63.28% of errors (GLM-OCR) to 79.21% (Gemini 3.5 Flash); Gemini 3.1 Pro is 77.51%, against 49.27% for human transcribers. On formulas it is 87.83-94.77% (Gemini 3.1 Pro 94.77%) against 46.36% for humans. Humans tend to mark unreadable text with '?' instead of guessing. This measure describes the type of error, not how many errors there are. Seong et al., 'When VLMs Fix Students' (arXiv 2604.22774), tested 15 models on FERMAT. They found that over-correction hides exactly the mistakes an assessment is meant to catch, with GPT-4o heavily penalised and Gemini 2.5 Flash the most faithful. The effect on marks is an inference: no paper measured inflated scores directly.

**Sources:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18586, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22959, https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22774

### 7. [medium] Recognisers trained on scanned handwriting fall apart on phone-camera handwriting, and training on camera data restores 38-44 points of word accuracy. ExamLeaf's own phone photos are therefore its most valuable data for testing and training.

**Evidence:** IIIT-Indic-HW-UC, Table 3 (ICPR 2024). Trained on the older scanned set and tested on camera images: Bengali WRR falls from 84.29 to 51.51 (CRR 95.72 to 79.66), and Hindi from 83.09 to 40.81 (CRR 96.08 to 72.27). Trained on camera data: Bengali 89.10 and Hindi 84.69, but accuracy on the old set drops to 53.82 and 63.65. Training on both sets is best on both. Other languages drop more (Malayalam 89.78 to 32.07, Tamil 92.19 to 41.24). Assamese exists only in the camera set (WRR 85.15). Confounds: camera conditions are mixed up with writer and content differences; the data are word crops from adults aged mostly 20-40; and the recogniser is an older-style baseline. The dataset and code were promised 'after acceptance' and the links are dead. A 2026 preprint (arXiv 2606.29213) found 9 of 10 OCR systems collapse on real Hindi scans: GPT-5.5 scored chrF++ 58.5 against 86.3 for Gemini 2.5 Flash.

**Sources:** https://cvit.iiit.ac.in/usodi/ucciohd.php, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-78495-8_21

### 11. [high] OpenRouter's default routing (data_collection: allow) can send children's answer sheets to providers that store prompts or train on them. Protecting them needs zdr:true, data_collection:deny and an allow-list of providers. The cheapest Qwen Flash models have no zero-retention endpoint at all.

**Evidence:** OpenRouter's docs say data_collection defaults to 'allow', which permits providers that store user data and may train on it. 'deny' restricts routing to providers that do not collect data. zdr:true routes only to endpoints with a Zero Data Retention (ZDR) policy. It is combined with account-wide, model-group and guardrail settings, and can only tighten them, never loosen them. OpenRouter's provider list (8 Oct 2026) flags 4 providers as training on data, including DeepSeek, and 39 as retaining prompts, including Google AI Studio (55 days), Anthropic (30 days), Xiaomi (30 days), Alibaba Cloud International, MiniMax and OpenAI. The FAQ says training providers are skipped unless an account setting is switched on. The ZDR endpoint list (945 endpoints) shows: GLM-5.3-Flash on 29 endpoints including Z.AI, DeepInfra, Together and Fireworks. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on 23 third-party hosts, but not DeepSeek's own. MiMo-V2.6-Pro on Novita and DeepInfra. Gemini 3.8 Flash and 3.1 Pro on Google. Claude Haiku 5.5 on Google and Amazon Bedrock. GPT-6 Luna on Azure. Qwen3-VL-235B on Venice, DeepInfra, Parasail and Novita. Qwen3.7-Flash and Qwen3.8-Flash: none. Whether each ZDR endpoint accepts images was not checked. ZDR does not mean data stays in India: OpenRouter's in-region routing covers only the EU and US. A BYOK key's 'declared_zdr' is self-declared, and ZDR does not cover plugins or tools.

Text grading after transcription: GLM-5.3-Flash ₹0.36 / ₹0.85 / ₹2.30; Gemini 3.8 Flash ₹2.18 / ₹5.80 / ₹16.68; Qwen3.7-Flash ₹0.08-₹0.58. At an assumed 20 papers per student per year, that is ₹55-₹170. Illustration only: if 6 of 30 answers go to a teacher at 30 seconds each and ₹300 per hour, review costs about ₹15 per paper, more than all the AI costs combined. Translating comments with Sarvam (₹0.005 per character) would cost ₹15-₹25 for 3,000-5,000 characters. Having the grader write comments directly in Assamese, Bengali or Hindi only adds output tokens. Caching the shared marking scheme (cached input is 10 times cheaper) saves little in absolute terms.

### 13. [medium] Adding Assamese or another Indic language 'easily' only works with open-weight models: fine-tune IndicOCR-class or GOT-OCR models with LoRA on your own phone photos. Commercial APIs come with a fixed language list. Feedback in the student's language is easiest to get by having the grader write it directly.

**Evidence:** There are three places a language can be added. 1. Reading. IndicPage-OCR trains one adapter per language on GOT-OCR 2.0. Adapters for Hindi, Bengali, Tamil and Telugu are public (Apache-2.0, Hugging Face SHAON123/indicpage-ocr-*); there is none for Assamese, and the code is 'coming soon'. Training used about 12M augmented word and line crops plus thousands of pages per language (for example 23,912 Hindi and 7,189 Bengali pages). The exact recipe and compute claim failed verification (1-2), but the data volume suggests labelled data, not GPUs, is the bottleneck. Bodhan IndicOCR (Qwen3.5-0.8B plus a 33M layout model, built with AI4Bharat) reads handwriting in English plus 12 Indian languages including Assamese. It is gated under a custom 'Indic Open Model License v1.0', so check commercial and fine-tuning terms; MLX and ONNX conversions exist. Training on phone-camera data matters a lot (38-44 WRR points, see the camera finding). Datasets: IIIT-Indic-HW-UC is camera-captured and includes Assamese, about 200K word images per language, but its release status is unclear. IIIT-INDIC-HW-WORDS is scanned and has no Assamese. Commercial APIs cannot be extended: Sarvam covers 22 Indic languages plus English; Google Document AI handwriting covers Bengali, Hindi, Marathi and Nepali; Cloud Vision has Assamese as experimental; Textract and Azure do not. 2. Grading. The prompt and marking scheme can be in any language, but Indic text uses more tokens. 3. Feedback. Sarvam translation costs ₹0.005 per character (₹0.004 on the Business plan), and Sarvam has a Dubbing API. Generating comments directly is cheaper. There is no checked evidence on how well GLM or Gemini write Assamese.

### 14. [low] Recommended first version, based on the evidence (this is the synthesizer's judgement, not a tested result). Run a two-stage, practice-only pipeline. Stage one: Gemini 3.8 Flash produces a word-for-word transcript, with low-confidence pages sent to Gemini 3.1 Pro, and the transcript is shown to the student. Stage two: GLM-5.3-Flash (or Gemini 3.8 Flash) grades it against the marking scheme. Route through OpenRouter with zdr:true, data_collection:deny and a provider allow-list. Before launch, test against ExamLeaf's own teacher-marked papers, with Sarvam Vision 2.1 and IndicOCR as the challengers.

**Evidence:** (1) Why two stages and not one. No checked evidence compares grading straight from the image with transcribing first. But silent correction can only be caught if the transcript is visible. A visible transcript lets the student or teacher flag misreads, lets ExamLeaf replace the reader and the grader separately as models expire, and costs only a few rupees more. Instruct the reader to transcribe word for word, keep the student's errors, write [?] for unreadable text instead of guessing, and split the output by question. (2) Reader. Use Gemini 3.8 Flash at high resolution (1,120 tokens per page). Send pages with many [?] marks to Gemini 3.1 Pro, the best measured reader of handwritten Assamese at 71.6% (note it is a preview model). Gemini was near the top on every handwriting test where it appeared in this research (Bodhan Indic, IIIT pages, WildHandBench, EDU-CIRCUIT-HW), and Gemini 2.5 Flash was the most faithful transcriber in Seong et al. Compare it with Sarvam Vision 2.1 Digitise (₹0.50 per page, Indian vendor, handwriting accuracy unpublished) and a self-hosted IndicOCR on ExamLeaf's own pages before committing. (3) Grader. If a Chinese model is wanted, use GLM-5.3-Flash: AA 41.8 at $0.15/$0.50, 29 ZDR endpoints including Z.AI, batch at $0.06/$0.20. Set its reasoning effort explicitly. Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna are alternatives in the same price band. Choose by agreement with teachers' marks, not by AA score. Avoid DeepSeek's own endpoint (it trains on data), Qwen3.7/3.8-Flash (no ZDR endpoint), Kimi K3 ($15 per million output tokens), the Gemini 2.5 and Qwen3-VL listings (expiring), Textract and Azure (no Assamese or Bengali), and Chinese OCR specialists for Indic scripts (no evidence, and they trail general models on handwriting). (4) Route. For a first version, use OpenRouter with zdr:true, data_collection:'deny', an explicit provider allow-list, exact model versions and account-wide ZDR. OpenRouter cannot keep data in India. (5) Product. Practice mode only, with marks labelled as estimates and a confidence level per answer. Send low-confidence, illegible, diagram and chemical-equation answers to a teacher review queue. Give students an appeal button. Before each release, require agreement with teachers on a held-out set of 100-200 marked papers per subject and language. (6) Consent. examleaf-web already records parental consent: accounts/models.py has parent_name, parent_contact and consent_at, plus a ConsentRecord model described as 'One consent event (DPDP Act)'. Extend the privacy notice to name AI processing of handwriting by named processors abroad, and get legal review. Cost: about ₹5.5 per paper in real time, ₹2.7 in batch and ₹8.4 with Pro re-reads, or ₹55-₹170 per student per year.

### 15. [medium] Risks the founder may not have considered: transcription that silently improves answers, frequent model retirements, unsafe default data routing, price jumps at size thresholds, runaway reasoning tokens, unmeasured accuracy for Assamese and diagrams, benchmarks run by vendors, and API rate limits at exam-season peaks.

**Evidence:** (a) Readers rewrite student errors into correct answers: 63-79% of errors are of this kind, and 88-95% on formulas, so marks can be inflated without any sign. (b) Model churn: 32 OpenRouter entries are expiring, some within 1-12 days of 8 Oct 2026, so an accuracy check done in one month may not hold the next. (c) OpenRouter's default data_collection 'allow' permits storing and training providers, and the cheapest Qwen Flash models have no ZDR endpoint. (d) Twelve page images can push a request past the 32K tier, costing 3.4 times as much on Qwen3.7-Flash. (e) GLM-5.3-Flash defaults to maximum reasoning: AA measured 46.6K reasoning tokens per task, all billed as output. (f) Nobody has measured Assamese exam handwriting, diagrams, chemical equations or crossed-out work. Readers trained mostly on Bengali may output Bengali র/ব instead of the Assamese letters ৰ/ৱ, so keywords would not match the marking scheme (inference). (g) The main benchmarks are run by vendors and change over time: Sarvam's was rewritten twice, and Bodhan's Sarvam figures predate Sarvam Vision 2.1. (h) Throughput: Sarvam Digitise allows 10 pages per job and 10 requests per minute. A 12-page paper needs 2 jobs, so one account can handle about 300 papers per hour or 7,200 per day unless enterprise limits are higher. OpenRouter's limits and batch turnaround times were not checked. (i) Gemini 3.1 Pro is a preview model. (j) IndicOCR uses a custom gated licence, not an OSI-approved open-source licence. (k) Using students' sheets to fine-tune models needs consent that covers it. (l) If transcripts can be edited and marks ever count for anything beyond practice, students can type in what they did not write. Lock the transcript and show the page image beside it (design inference). (m) Leniency, bias and invented marks were not measured for any grading model.

## Caveats

1. Snapshot. Prices, model lists and zero-retention endpoints were read on 7-8 Oct 2026, and they change weekly. Several models expire between 8 and 21 Oct.

2. No source measures ExamLeaf's real input: phone photos of Class 10/12 answer sheets in Assamese, Bengali or Hindi, with maths, chemical equations, diagrams and crossed-out work. Every accuracy figure is a stand-in: prose pages, word crops, printed text, or Chinese/English handwriting.

3. Most Indic accuracy figures were published by vendors (Sarvam's benchmark, Bodhan's IndicOCR-HW) or come from test sets that have not been released (IIIT IndicPage, OmniHandwritingOCR, WildHandBench). None has been independently reproduced.
   - Per-language samples are small (Assamese 471, Bengali 271, Hindi 228 on Sarvam's benchmark), so gaps of 1-2 points are noise.
   - Bodhan's Sarvam Vision figures predate Sarvam Vision 2.1.

4. Nothing about grading quality survived verification. No checked evidence exists on:
   - automated short-answer or essay scoring;
   - checking maths steps;
   - following a marking scheme;
   - leniency or bias;
   - grading straight from the image versus transcribing first;
   - Sarvam-M.

   The Artificial Analysis index used to compare models is a general-intelligence score at maximum effort. Scores at default effort are lower: Claude Sonnet 5.5 drops from 56 to 46.8, and GPT-6 Luna from 38.1 to 29.9.

5. Also unverified or not covered:
   - self-hosting costs on E2E Networks, Yotta, AWS Mumbai or Google Mumbai;
   - Mistral OCR and Bhashini;
   - Azure and Textract per-page prices;
   - latency and batch turnaround;
   - existing AI answer-sheet checkers, and what boards or teacher bodies have said about them;
   - human-in-the-loop designs used in practice.

   The DPDP Act was not verified in this run. From background knowledge only: the Act requires verifiable parental consent before processing data of anyone under 18, and lets the government restrict transfers to countries it names. Its rules were notified in late 2025 and come into force in phases. Get legal advice before sending minors' handwriting to foreign processors.

6. I added some facts after verification, each from a single fetch and not cross-checked:
   - OpenRouter batch prices and the zero-retention endpoint list;
   - Sarvam's ₹0.50/page and ₹0.005/character prices;

   - the reference to ExamLeaf's consent model in accounts/models.py.

7. The cost model rests on assumed token counts:
   - marking scheme 4,000 tokens;

   - 1,120-2,560 tokens per page image;
