# ExamLeaf product ideas: sealed sample papers, QR-gated free solutions with registration, 1…

This run has verified evidence for ideas 1 to 3 only. Ideas 4 (multi-board market) and 5 (AI answer checking) produced no surviving claims, so recommendations on them would rest on two earlier, separate runs in docs/research. Sealed exam packs already exist: Educart's 2026-27 CBSE Class 10 books seal 2 of their 15 papers as 'pre-board' sets, and Korea's EBS sells an envelope mock exam every year. The learning research supports regular practice on board-pattern papers with corrective feedback (quizzing g about 0.50; matched format 0.53 vs 0.40; with feedback 0.54 vs 0.37). It says nothing about the seal itself, and the cost of sealing and its effect on bookshop sales and returns were not verified. Putting solutions behind a QR scan plus student registration creates three problems. It weakens the feedback that the evidence supports. Once the DPDP duties start (about 13 May 2027), it makes ExamLeaf a fiduciary of children's data that needs identity-checked parental consent, with no publisher exemption. And it reaches rural Assam teenagers who mostly use a family phone, of whom only about half could produce a connected phone and complete a web search. On videos, edX course logs favour segments of about 6 minutes or less, and one small experiment says chapter navigation matters more than length. Meanwhile PhysicsWallah's national and Assam channels draw their audiences with live revision sessions of 2 to 12 hours, so a 15-minute chapter format is an untested bet. The course the evidence supports: 1. Print answers or a marking scheme in the book. 2. Keep detailed solutions free and open, with no student accounts. 3. Seal only a final pre-board set, if anything. 4. Pilot short, chaptered videos with built-in questions before scaling.

## Findings

### 1. [high] Sealed or envelope-style exam packs already exist, but only in limited form. Educart's 2026-27 CBSE Class 10 sample-paper books include two sealed pre-board sets among their 15 papers. South Korea's state-owned broadcaster EBS sells a branded 'envelope mock exam' (봉투모의고사) for the CSAT as a yearly product. Neither source shows how the seal is made, what it costs or whether it improves results.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [0] and [1].

Educart: its campaign page (byline 26 Sep 2026) and its product pages say each 2026-27 CBSE Class 10 book (Science, Maths Standard, English, Social Science) has 15 sample papers, including 'two sealed pre-board paper sets with answer sheets'. Amazon.in buyer photos show Sample Papers 14 and 15 listed as unsolved sealed sets, plus two blank answer-writing sheets with OMR and a loose replica board paper with red SEAL tabs. Verified-purchase reviews run from 28 Sep to 4 Oct 2026. The 'answer sheets' are blank writing sheets, not solutions. The seal looks like a sticker or printed band on loose inserts, and no source mentions perforation, a glued edge or shrink-wrap.

EBS: the 2024-CSAT edition page lists 2025, 2026 and 2027 editions, and the notice board announced another envelope series on 28 Sep 2026. Aladin lists the 2027 subject books at KRW 9,500-13,000. Private Korean publishers sell their own envelope mock exams, so it is an established genre there. A separate claim about what the EBS envelope contains (a same-format paper and an enclosed OMR card) was refuted 1-2, so those details should not be relied on.

**Sources:** https://www.educart.co/news/educart-cbse-class-10-sample-papers-40-off-2026-27, https://www.ebsi.co.kr/ebs/pot/potg/retrieveSeriesSubjectList.ebs?seriesGrpId=PKG_0262&seriesId=PRO_1736

### 2. [high] Practice testing reliably raises achievement. A 2021 meta-analysis of 222 classroom studies (48,478 students) found a medium effect, g = 0.499, with no noteworthy publication bias. Testing beats re-reading (g = 0.33) but is only marginally better than elaborative methods such as concept mapping or summarising (g = 0.095, not significant). Effects were larger in high school (g = 0.655), and repeated testing beat a single test.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [2] and [16]. Source: Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu and Shanks, Psychological Bulletin 147(4):399-435 (2021). Crossref shows no correction.

Pooled result: 573 effect sizes, 95% CI 0.442-0.557, 82.9% of effects positive, substantial heterogeneity (Q(572) = 4,816). Five bias checks gave adjusted estimates of 0.427-0.475.

By comparison condition: no or filler activity 0.610, restudying 0.330, elaborative strategies 0.095 (p = .062).

Subgroups from the verifier's reading:
- high school k = 49, g = 0.655
- Biology 0.409, Chemistry 0.340, Maths/statistics 0.433
- one test 0.444 versus unlimited repetitions 0.762 (k = 28)
- quizzes outside class 0.401 versus in class 0.514 (p = .051)

Other meta-analyses agree in direction: Sotola and Crede 2021 (d = 0.42) and Bangert-Drowns et al. 1991 (about 0.5 SD).

Limits: the studies run 1929-2019, 58% of effects come from universities, and the content had to be taught in class. None involve Indian board exams, so home study from a printed book is an extrapolation.

**Sources:** https://gwern.net/doc/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf, https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/

### 3. [medium] Exam-like stakes and the delivery medium did not change the testing benefit, but matching the practice format to the final exam did (g = 0.531 matched vs 0.399 mismatched). The review has no moderator for sealed or tear-out packaging or for timed full-length mock exams. So the seal itself is untested, and the evidence supports papers that faithfully copy the board pattern.

**Evidence:** Claim [4].

Results:
- High-stake g = 0.441 (k = 160) versus low-stake 0.477 (k = 135), p = .462.
- Administration mode not significant, Q(6) = 4.297, p = .637 (pen-and-paper 0.494, web 0.447, clicker 0.549).
- Matched format 0.531 (k = 328) versus mismatched 0.399 (k = 151), Q(1) = 19.633, p < .001.
- Recall and recognition tests equal (0.520 vs 0.518).

No moderator covers timed, full-length, mock, sealed or tear-out formats. The 'Simulation' mode (k = 6) means clinical-scenario quizzes.

Disagreements: format matching was not significant in Rowland (2014) but was favoured in Adesope et al. (2017). Yang's own summary table contradicts its Results text on test format.

The stakes result does not show that an exam feel helps. The authors suggest anxiety may cancel out motivation, and Khanna (2015) found ungraded quizzes beat graded ones on the final exam (a verifier lead). Most effects come from short university quizzes, not 3-hour papers.

**Sources:** https://gwern.net/doc/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf

### 4. [medium] The testing benefit is larger when students get corrective feedback (g = 0.537 with vs 0.374 without, p = .001), although earlier meta-analyses disagree on how much. For weak students feedback may be decisive: without it, the testing effect was close to nil when initial scores were 50% or below. Putting solutions behind a QR scan plus registration therefore adds friction to the part of practice that matters most for low scorers. That last point is an inference; no study tests access friction.

**Evidence:** Claim [3].

Yang et al. moderator: feedback k = 335, g = 0.537 [0.469, 0.604]; no feedback k = 142, g = 0.374 [0.278, 0.471]; Q(1) = 10.849, p = .001. The authors recommend that feedback routinely follow quizzing.

The verifier checked the earlier meta-analyses in the original:
- Rowland (2014): 0.73 with feedback versus 0.39 without.
- Adesope et al. (2017): 0.63 versus 0.60, not significant.
- Rowland also found that without feedback the effect was g = 0.03 when initial scores were 50% or below, against 0.56 above 75%.

Qualifications:
- These are between-study comparisons, one moderator at a time.
- No study tests access friction or how quickly feedback arrives. Rowland's small subset even favoured delayed feedback.
- The feedback increment ranges from 0.03 to 0.34 SD across the three meta-analyses.
- Practice without feedback still helps (0.374).

The cost of gating falls on students who never open the solutions. No data here can size that group.

**Sources:** https://gwern.net/doc/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf

### 5. [high] Oswaal does not rely on a QR code alone for book-linked solutions. Buyers scratch a code on the back cover, scan a QR code on the last paper to reach Oswaal's site, log in, and enter the single-use code. Oswaal applies this gate to only a minority of current titles, and the gate ties content to a purchased copy rather than collecting leads. No verified evidence was found on how Arihant, Educart, MTG, S. Chand or Xam Idea deliver digital solutions.

**Evidence:** Claim [8].

The FAQ describes the process: scratch the back-cover code, scan the QR code on the last self-assessment paper, log in with ID and password or mobile number, and enter the code.

The verifier's check of the live site on 8 Oct 2026 found that the scratch-card page:
- forces login
- rejects already-used codes
- sends the browser's latitude and longitude with the code

The gate flag is set for only 21 of 138 Class 12 sample-paper entries and 33 of 181 Class 12 question-bank entries. It is set for none of the One-for-All or Class 11 sample-paper entries. The 2026-27 sample-paper editions are marketed with 'Oswaal AI' and no QR or scratch code, so the mechanism may be changing.

Oswaal's privacy policy has only an under-13 clause, not the DPDP under-18 standard. The FAQ is undated and partly stale.

**Sources:** https://oswaalbooks.com/community/faq

### 6. [high] India's DPDP Rules 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E), 13 Nov 2025) start in stages. Consent Manager registration (Rule 4) begins about 13 Nov 2026. Notice, security, breach, retention, children's consent, data-principal rights and cross-border rules (Rules 3, 5-16, 22 and 23) begin about 13 May 2027, roughly seven months after 8 Oct 2026. The Act's children's section (s.9) starts on the same date.

**Evidence:** Claim [5], checked against Rule 1(2)-(4) in the Gazette and the companion notice G.S.R. 843(E). That notice brings the Act's sections 3-5 and 7-17, including s.9, into force at 18 months.

Could the date move? MeitY floated a 12-month window in Jan-Mar 2026, and IAMAI objected that verifiable children's consent cannot be built that fast. But Fortune India (14 Aug 2026) reported the IT Secretary saying the notified timelines would hold. Business Standard (11 Sep 2026), India Briefing (10 Sep 2026) and Hindu BusinessLine (4-5 Oct 2026) all use 13 May 2027.

The DPDP (Removal of Difficulties) Order 2026, gazetted 7 Oct 2026, changed two phrases and no dates. India Briefing's '13 March 2027' (7 Oct 2026) contradicts both Gazette notices and is almost certainly a typo. The date may be 13 or 14 May, because the Gazette was uploaded on 14 Nov.

Inference: student accounts created for the Feb-Mar 2027 exams will still be held when the duties start. How pre-commencement child accounts are treated was not checked.

**Sources:** https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf

### 7. [high] From that date, registering any under-18 student requires verifiable parental consent before any personal data is processed. The fiduciary must also check that the person consenting is an identifiable adult. That check uses identity and age details it already holds, details the person provides, or a virtual token from an authorised entity such as DigiLocker. The Rules list no lighter route, such as a phone OTP or self-declaration.

**Evidence:** Claim [6], checked against Gazette pp. 27-28.

Rule 10(1) requires measures to obtain verifiable parental consent before processing a child's data. The due-diligence check relies on reliable identity and age details already held, or on details provided voluntarily or through a virtual token. Rule 10(2) defines an adult as 18 or over and names Digital Locker service providers among authorised entities. 'Child' comes from the Act (under 18). The Rule's illustrations point first-time parents to government-issued details, a virtual token or DigiLocker.

Qualifications:
- Identifiability is tied to compliance with other laws.
- The voluntarily-provided route is not expressly government-verified.
- A vendor blog (Consently, May 2026) reads Rule 10 as requiring proof of the parent-child relationship. The text does not.

Inference: a site that makes Class 10-12 students register must build identity-checked parental consent, plus notice, security, breach reporting, retention and data-rights handling.

**Sources:** https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf

### 8. [medium] The exemptions from parental consent and from the tracking ban cover only a closed list:
- healthcare providers
- educational institutions, and only for tracking linked to their own teaching or the safety of enrolled children
- creches and school transport
- a few narrow purposes

Publishers, exam-prep and edtech platforms are not named, so ExamLeaf should plan on no exemption. Separately, s.9(3) of the Act bars tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children, and parental consent cannot lift that bar.

**Evidence:** Claim [7], checked against Rule 12 and the Fourth Schedule (pp. 29 and 36-38).

Part A lists healthcare establishments and professionals, educational institutions, creches and school-transport providers. The educational-institution entry would not cover account registration even if ExamLeaf qualified.

Part B has six purposes, not the four in the original claim. The verifier added duties under any law and statutory or state-funded services.

'Educational institution' is defined inclusively ('an institution of learning that imparts education'). MeitY's January 2026 summary of submissions shows stakeholders asked whether ed-tech platforms count. The final text gives no answer, so 'no exemption' is a prudent planning assumption, not settled law. No s.9(5) 'verifiably safe' age notification exists.

The targeted-advertising wording of s.9(3) is from the Act text and was not re-verified in this run.

**Sources:** https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf

### 9. [high] In rural Assam almost every household has a smartphone, but few school-age students own one.
- ASER 2024, ages 14-16: 91.4% have a smartphone at home, but only 14.6% of those who can use one own it. That is about 12% of all 14-16-year-olds (girls 11.4% vs boys 18.5%).
- Older students: ownership was 65.6% at ages 17-18 in rural Kamrup (ASER 2023), and ASER's national grade-wise figure is 42.7% for Std XI-XII.

Class 10 students would mostly reach a QR link on a parent's phone. Class 12 students would use their own phone roughly half the time.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [9] and [11].

ASER 2024 rural Assam (26 of 27 districts; Table 10, p. 88; self-reported): smartphone at home 91.4%, can use one 85.1%. Ownership among users is 14.6% overall: 9.7% at age 14, 14.2% at 15 and 21.8% at 16.

ASER 2023 rural Kamrup (1,320 youth, 60 villages): at home 91.3%. Ownership among users is 17.6% at 14-16 (girls 11.8%, boys 25.1%), 65.6% at 17-18 (girls 56.3%, boys 77.1%) and 36.0% overall.

ASER's national table (28 districts, ages 14-18 pooled, not age-matched) gives own-phone rates of 15.7% for Std X or below and 42.7% for Std XI-XII. So the Class 12 rate is somewhere between about 43% and 66%, depending on the measure.

Assam's 14.6% is close to West Bengal (13.4%), Meghalaya (13.8%) and Odisha (21.5%); the India figure is 31.4%.

Limits: rural only, ages rather than grades, no urban data, 2023-24 fieldwork. National ownership at ages 14-16 rose from about 19% to 31% between ASER's 2023 pilot and 2024, so these figures probably understate 2026-27.

**Sources:** https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Assam-1.pdf, https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/3_Assam_Kamrup.pdf

### 10. [high] Being able to use a connected phone is much less common than having one at home.
- Rural Assam, ages 14-16 (ASER 2024): only 73.0% could bring a smartphone with good connectivity, and about 50% could both bring one and complete a web search.
- Rural Kamrup, ages 17-18 (ASER 2023): about 45% could do both.

Finding a named YouTube video was easier (86-91% of those with a phone) than browsing for information (48-69%). Only 21-42% had ever used an online service such as filling a form. ASER did not test QR scanning or registration.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [10] and [12].

ASER 2024 Assam (Table 14), ages 14-16: 73.0% could bring a connected phone. Of those, 73.7% set an alarm, 69.1% found an answer by web search and 87.2% found a named YouTube video. About 50% could both bring a phone and search (boys 53.5%, girls 47.9%).

ASER 2023 Kamrup: 70.5% could bring a connected phone (65.3% at 14-16, 78.8% at 17-18). Among 17-18-year-olds who could: YouTube 91.1%, alarm 74.0%, browsing 56.9%, Google Maps 33.1%. About 45% could both bring a phone and browse. Self-reported 'ever used an online service such as paying or filling a form' was 21.4% at 14-16 and 41.9% at 17-18.

Caveats:
- The YouTube task supplied the video title, while the browse task needed a query built from an Assamese question. So 'video is easier' is confounded with task design.
- Kamrup's weak browsing is partly local: the 2024 statewide browse rate is 69.1%, against Kamrup's 47.7% at 14-16.
- Survey-day results are floors.

No direct data on registration conversion exist.

**Sources:** https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Assam-1.pdf, https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/3_Assam_Kamrup.pdf

### 11. [high] In 6.9 million viewing sessions from four 2012 edX math and science courses, students watched at most about 6 minutes per session (median) whatever the video's length. They often stopped before halfway in videos over 9 minutes. The share who attempted the follow-up problem fell from 56% for videos of 0-3 minutes to 31% for 12-40 minutes. A 15-minute video falls in the worst group (about 3 minutes watched per session, median). This measures engagement among self-motivated adult learners, not learning or exam results.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [13], [14] and [15]. Source: Guo, Kim and Rubin, ACM Learning at Scale 2014, about 2,100 citations.

Data: 862 videos, 127,839 students, normal-speed sessions only (76% of all). In 0-3 minute videos, 75% of sessions watched more than three quarters of the video. For the 32% of videos followed by a problem, problem attempts within 30 minutes were 56%, 48%, 43%, 41% and 31% across the five length groups.

The authors' own limits: retrospective logs, not an experiment; early-adopter learners; engagement proxies only; they warn against drawing conclusions about learning. No school-age or Indian students were studied.

Disagreement (verifier leads, not voted separately):
- Lagerstrom et al. (Stanford, 2015): committed for-credit students watched 12-13 minutes per session, and 90-100% of long videos across sessions.
- Geri et al. (2017): questions embedded in videos of 11 minutes or more raised viewing time and completion.

Exam-motivated students and interactive videos may therefore behave differently.

**Sources:** https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf

### 12. [low] One small experiment (Seidel 2024, 22 adults, videos of 50-66 minutes) concludes that length matters less than splitting content into meaningful segments. A single video with a chapter menu gave a higher learning gain than a plain video (7.43 vs 5.29, p = .018). Cutting the video into a series of separate shorter videos made no significant difference (p = .18). Short versus long videos were never compared directly.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [21] and [22]. Source: Technology, Knowledge and Learning 29:1965-1991 (July 2024), open access with open data; the verifier re-ran the tests on the published data.

Study 2: a 66-minute lecture and a 50-minute documentary, a 10-question multiple-choice test before and after, and 22 of 38 adult starters analysed (7-8 per group).

Learning gain:
- Chaptered single video beat plain (p = .018).
- Series of videos (6.63) versus plain: not significant.
- Chaptered versus series: not significant (p = .52).
- Three-group test: not significant (p = .080).

The abstract's 'segmented videos resulted in higher learning gains' overstates the series result. The paper's post-test claim for the series is not supported by its own data. 'Length is not crucial' is an inference, because length was never varied.

In direction this matches Rey et al.'s 2019 meta-analysis, which found small-to-medium segmenting benefits (a verifier lead). The study gives weak support for chapter navigation inside revision videos, and nothing on school students or 15-minute videos.

**Sources:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10758-024-09745-2

### 14. [medium] View counts point the same way, although they measure demand, not learning. NCERT Wallah's 30 most-viewed videos have a median length of about 2 h 37 min and 1.5M to 9.2M views each. On PW Assam HS Science, median views were:
- about 12.5K for the eight half-yearly marathons
- about 2.5K for One Shot streams
- about 1.6K for clips of 10 minutes or less
- about 607 for six compressed 'full chapter in 25-40 minutes' videos

For the same chapters, the live One Shot outdrew the compressed video 2.9 to 9 times. Shorts, however, draw very large audiences.

**Evidence:** Merges claims [18] and [20].

NCERT Wallah, sorted by popularity: the top 30 run 1:00:26 to 6:49:40. 20 of them are 2 hours or longer, and none is 30 minutes or shorter. The top video is a 1:28:24 'Solutions' revision with 9,245,274 views.

PW Assam HS Science: the most-viewed marathons are Physics (about 20K), Biology (17K), Chemistry (14K) and Maths Part 1 (13K). The 'Most Important Short Questions' live classes drew about 6.95K, and the compressed videos 404-919 views each.

Confounders:
- Live streams are promoted.
- The marathons ran 20-26 Sep, just before half-yearly exams, while the compressed videos were posted in Jul-Aug.
- Only six compressed videos exist.
- Many top NCERT Wallah videos are 2-5 years old.

Counter-signals:
- An NCERT Wallah Short has about 11M views, and about 18 Shorts beat the 30th long video.
- Among NCERT Wallah's 750 most recent uploads, 34% are 30 minutes or shorter (median 71K views, against 183K for longer ones).
- PW Assam Shorts have a median of about 4.8K views, above the One Shot median.

### 15. [medium] Recommendation for solutions (an inference from the findings above): do not make worked solutions depend on a QR scan plus student registration. Instead:
1. Print at least final answers or a compact marking scheme for every paper in the book.
2. Put the detailed step-by-step solutions on an open page for each paper, with no login, reached by the QR code and a printed short URL.
3. Keep any account optional and held by the parent.

This keeps the feedback that drives the practice benefit and avoids taking on child-data duties. It also suits a market where most Class 10 students use a parent's phone and only about half can complete a simple web task.

**Evidence:** Why:
- Feedback lifts practice most, especially for weak scorers (claim 3).
- Compulsory student accounts become a regulated child-data operation from about 13 May 2027, with identity-checked parental consent and no publisher exemption (claims 5-7).
- About 45-50% of rural teenagers could produce a connected phone and complete a search, and only 21-42% had ever filled in an online form (claims 9-12).
- Even Oswaal gates only a minority of titles, and gates them to tie content to a purchase (claim 8).

Print the QR code to point at a redirect on ExamLeaf's own domain, so the target can change between reprints. This is standard practice but was not verified in this run.

Trade-offs:
- Open solutions will be copied. No evidence was found on whether that hurts sales, and solutions that are free anyway lose little.
- Without accounts ExamLeaf gets no customer list, only aggregate page counts. A lawyer should confirm those are fine under s.9(3).
- If accounts are wanted later, make the parent the account holder and design consent around DigiLocker or similar tokens.
- A short printed answer key costs a few pages.

**Sources:** https://gwern.net/doc/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf, https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf, https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Assam-1.pdf, https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/3_Assam_Kamrup.pdf, https://oswaalbooks.com/community/faq

### 16. [low] Recommendation for sealed papers (an inference from the findings above): treat the seal as packaging, not as a teaching tool.
- If ExamLeaf uses it, seal only the last two or three papers as a 'pre-board' set, for example as loose sealed inserts in Educart's style, so the bound book can still be browsed in shops.
- Put the main effort into exact ASSEB paper pattern, timing and self-marking against a marking scheme. That is what the evidence supports.
- Get printer and bookseller quotes before committing.

**Evidence:** The precedent shows buyers and sellers accept the format in India and Korea. The research favours format-matched practice (g 0.531 vs 0.399) and feedback, finds that stakes make no difference, and says nothing about seals or timed full-length papers.

Risks that were asked about but not verified in this run:
- Customers cannot leaf through sealed pages in a bookshop.
- Booksellers may refuse returns of copies with broken seals.
- Perforation or a glued edge adds cost and can weaken the binding.
- An exam feel may raise anxiety rather than effort (Yang et al.'s discussion; Khanna 2015, a verifier lead).

**Sources:** https://www.educart.co/news/educart-cbse-class-10-sample-papers-40-off-2026-27, https://www.ebsi.co.kr/ebs/pot/potg/retrieveSeriesSubjectList.ebs?seriesGrpId=PKG_0262&seriesId=PRO_1736, https://gwern.net/doc/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf

### 17. [low] Recommendation for videos (an inference from the findings above): if ExamLeaf makes chapter videos, build each 15-minute chapter as short segments (about 6 minutes or less each) with a chapter menu and built-in practice questions linked to the book's papers. Publish them on YouTube, where students already are. Pilot a few chapters and measure completion and question attempts before scaling to three languages. Position the videos as quick last-week revision alongside PW's free multi-hour live sessions, not against them.

**Evidence:** Why:
- In course logs, engagement falls after about 6-9 minutes, so even 15 minutes is 'long'.
- Chapter navigation helped in one small experiment, and embedded questions raised viewing in another study (a verifier lead).
- Retrieval practice with feedback has the strongest learning evidence of anything reviewed here.
- In Assam, the market leader's free long live revisions draw far more views than its compressed chapter videos, though that comparison is confounded.
- Finding a named YouTube video was the digital task rural teenagers completed most often (86-91%).

Production cost, tooling and AI-dubbing quality for Assamese, Bengali and Hindi were not verified in this run.

Evidence quality
- Learning research: mostly short university classroom quizzes up to 2019, with only 49 high-school effects and no Indian board data. The meta-analyses disagree on how large the feedback and format-matching effects are.
- Video research: engagement data from 2012 adult online courses, plus one 22-person experiment.
- Device data: rural only, self-reported, by age rather than grade, from 2023-24 fieldwork in a fast-changing market. There is no direct data on QR scanning or registration conversion.
- YouTube figures: a single-day snapshot (8 Oct 2026) of pages that change. Views are confounded by promotion, live format, exam timing and video age.
- Unvoted verifier notes: several were gathered during verification but not voted on separately, including the Gyanam Tuition and Pythagoras Classes lengths, Lagerstrom 2015, Geri 2017, Rey 2019 and Khanna 2015.
- Search limits: the verifiers' web-search budgets ran out, so contradiction checks relied on direct fetches and scholarly APIs.

Time-sensitivity
- The DPDP dates could still be pulled forward. MeitY floated a 12-month window in early 2026, but nothing was notified.

- Educart's sealed sets are a 2026-27 edition feature.
- ASER phone-ownership figures rise year on year.
